# Mapping the Full Landscape for a Cross-Linguistic Catalog of Open Monosyllabic Words

## TL;DR
- The single-language descriptive catalog the user already specced (onset complexity, nucleus type, tone, gloss/function) is well-supported by mature, citable standards — adopt CLDF (Forkel et al. 2018), Glottolog, Concepticon, CLTS, and Moran & Cysouw's orthography profiles/`segments` for transcription handling, and treat PHOIBLE (Moran & McCloy 2019) and WALS Chapter 12 (Maddieson 2013) as the typological backbone.
- The far richer comparative/quantitative possibilities the original spec excluded — phonetic edit distance (LingPy SCA/LexStat), colexification (CLICS), sound symbolism (Blasi et al. 2016), cognate detection, homophony/information-theory analyses — are all methodologically feasible on the same data but carry a serious, well-documented risk: short open monosyllables are exactly where chance resemblance is highest (Ringe 1992), so any cross-linguistic "look-alike" claim needs an explicit chance baseline.
- The biggest structural caveat is upstream: word-minimality/bimoraicity constraints (McCarthy & Prince 1986) mean many languages *systematically ban* open light monosyllables, so the catalog's per-language yield will be highly uneven and correlated with syllable-structure type and tone.

## Key Findings
- **Standards are settled.** CLDF + its reference catalogs (Glottolog, Concepticon, CLTS) plus Lexibank tooling (PyLexibank, CLDFBench, CL Toolkit) constitute a de facto standard for reproducible cross-linguistic lexical data; CL Toolkit already auto-extracts syllable-complexity and colexification features.
- **IPA sourcing is tractable but noisy.** Wikipron, Wiktextract/Kaikki, NorthEuraLex, and Epitran G2P can supply IPA at scale, but each has documented error profiles (e.g., English WikiPron shows 153 "phonemes," far too many).
- **Syllabification and contested analyses are the hard core.** Diphthong-vs-VG, syllabic consonants as nuclei, and tone/nasality handling are genuine analytic forks; the literature (Easterday 2019; sonority-sequencing debates) shows there is no framework-neutral answer, so decisions must be documented, not assumed.
- **The data supports a large analysis menu.** Beyond description: Zipf/Piantadosi word-length–information analyses, homophony-rate analyses (Trott & Bergen 2020), phonotactic probability/neighborhood density (Vitevitch & Luce 1999), and the whole comparative stack (SCA/LexStat, ASJP, CLICS, Blasi-style sound–meaning biases).
- **Chance resemblance is the recurring methodological trap** for anything comparative on short words, quantified since Ringe (1992) and operationalized in LingPy-based chance-resemblance work (Tresoldi 2019).

## Details

### 1. METHODOLOGY

**Phonological typology of syllable structure.** The anchor is Ian Maddieson's WALS Chapter 12, "Syllable Structure" (Dryer & Haspelmath, eds., *WALS Online*, 2013), which classifies languages as simple (only CV-type), moderately complex, or complex. In Maddieson's chapter text, the counts are precise: "Only 61 (about 12.5%) of the languages in the sample limit themselves to nothing more complicated than the universal syllable type, CV"; "moderately complex syllable structure, accounting for about 56.5% of the sample" (274 languages); and "150 languages (about 30.9% of the total) permit complex syllables." Crucially for this project, only the "simple" group is dominated by open CV syllables, so open monosyllables will be most abundant there. Maddieson also quantifies the link between syllable complexity and consonant-inventory size: "Across the set of 484 languages for which both consonant inventory size and syllable structure data are included, there is a significant though not strong correlation... the correlation coefficient is .203" — larger inventories tend to co-occur with more complex syllables. The definitive monograph on the complex end is Shelece Easterday's *Highly Complex Syllable Structure: A Typological and Diachronic Study* (Language Science Press, 2019; based on her 2017 UNM dissertation), a 100-language sample (25 highly complex) that also shows how complex syllables arise diachronically through vowel reduction — the mirror image of the open-monosyllable-rich languages this catalog will favor. The LAPSyD database (Lyon-Albuquerque Phonological Systems Database, Maddieson et al.) covers ~700 languages with syllable-structure, stress, and tone data and descends from UPSID/WALS.

The theoretical scaffolding for onset/rhyme/nucleus and cluster well-formedness is the Sonority Sequencing Principle (Sievers 1876; Jespersen 1904; Hooper 1976; Selkirk 1982; Clements 1990), with the standard sonority scale vowels > glides > liquids > nasals > obstruents, and Steve Parker's work (2002, 2011, *The Sonority Controversy* 2012) as the modern reference. Important caveat the user should absorb: a large cross-linguistic study in *Linguistic Typology* (2022), examining 496 languages, finds that "almost one half of the language sample" violates the SSP word-initially or word-finally — so sonority is a tendency, not a law. For open monosyllables (no coda) this mostly matters for onset-cluster (CCV, CCCV) classification.

**Cross-linguistic database standards.** The core citation is Forkel, List, Greenhill, Bank, Rzymski, Cysouw, Hammarström, Haspelmath, Kaiping & Gray (2018), "Cross-Linguistic Data Formats, advancing data sharing and re-use in comparative linguistics," *Scientific Data* 5:180205. CLDF unifies lexical data along language/concept/form and links to reference catalogs: Glottolog (Hammarström et al., languages), Concepticon (List et al., concept sets), and CLTS/Cross-Linguistic Transcription Systems (List, Anderson et al., speech sounds). Lexibank (List et al. 2022, *Scientific Data*; Lexibank 2 / Blum et al. 2025) is a repository of 100+ CLDF datasets, more than 4,000 wordlists from more than 2,400 language varieties, with a companion CL Toolkit that automatically extracts phonological features including syllable complexity and colexifications. PHOIBLE 2.0 (Moran & McCloy, eds., 2019) provides 3,020 inventories / 3,183 segment types / 2,186 languages in standardized Unicode IPA — the natural source for validating which nucleus types (long vowels, nasal vowels, diphthongs, syllabic consonants) and tones a given language contrasts. The whole ecosystem is published via the clld web framework (Forkel & Bank 2014) and follows FAIR principles (Wilkinson et al. 2016).

**IPA sourcing and processing.** Several scalable sources exist: (a) WikiPron (Lee et al. 2020, LREC), a tool + database mining IPA from Wiktionary — the LREC paper reports "an automatically-generated database of 1.7 million pronunciations from 165 languages," while the current WikiPron repository (github.com/CUNY-CL/wikipron) states "a database of over 3 million word/pronunciation pairs"; the paper flags quality problems (English shows 153 unique "phonemes," implying inconsistency). (b) Wiktextract/Kaikki (Ylönen 2022, LREC, pp. 1317–1325), which fully expands Wiktionary templates/Lua to produce JSON including pronunciations. (c) NorthEuraLex (Dellert et al. 2020, *Language Resources and Evaluation* 54:273–301), 1,016 concepts × 107 languages of Northern Eurasia in unified, automatically-generated IPA. (d) PanLex (Kamholz, Pool & Colowick 2014, LREC) for panlingual lexeme coverage (~20M lexemes / ~9,000 varieties as of the 2014 paper) but weak on IPA. For languages without pronunciation data, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion is available: Epitran (Mortensen, Dalmia & Littell 2018, LREC) is rule-based for many languages; neural/zero-shot G2P (Transphone; Li et al. 2022) extends to thousands; G2P accuracy varies enormously by orthographic depth (shallow orthographies like Finnish/Hungarian are near-perfect; deep ones like English/Korean are error-prone). This is directly relevant: G2P errors concentrate exactly in the vowel/final-segment decisions that determine whether a word counts as "open."

**IPA segmentation/tokenization.** The standard is Moran & Cysouw's *The Unicode Cookbook for Linguists: Managing Writing Systems Using Orthography Profiles* (2018, Language Science Press) and its `segments` Python library (implements orthography-profile tokenization), used by WikiPron and Lexibank. LingPy provides IPA tokenization, sound-class conversion, and prosodic-string/sonority-profile generation. A documented limitation: the `segments` library historically mis-segments pre-posed diacritics (e.g., pre-aspiration attached to the preceding vowel) — a real concern for nucleus classification.

**Operationalizing contested decisions.** The user's spec fields all map onto genuine, unresolved analytic choices: diphthong vs vowel+glide (affects whether a "nucleus type" is a diphthong or the syllable is actually CVG/closed); syllabic consonants as nuclei (whether e.g. a syllabic nasal counts as an "open monosyllable"); tone and nasality as features of the nucleus vs the syllable. Easterday (2019) explicitly notes that syllable-structure frameworks are often pre-imposed and "the patterns of the language are made to fit into the model," complicating cross-linguistic comparison. The practical recommendation from the CLTS/CLDF world is to fix a transcription level (phonemic "BroadIPA" via CLTS) and document every decision in an orthography profile.

**Word minimality / why some languages ban open light monosyllables.** This is the most important upstream constraint and it is the subject of a large prosodic-morphology literature: McCarthy & Prince (1986 et seq.) derive the "Minimal Word" from the Prosodic Hierarchy + Foot Binarity — a prosodic word must contain a foot, feet are minimally bimoraic, so a minimal word is bimoraic. In quantity-sensitive languages this bans open *light* (CV, short-vowel) monosyllables outright, or triggers augmentation/lengthening (documented for Lardil, Māori, Axininca Campa, etc.). Implication for the catalog: languages differ categorically in whether open light monosyllables can even exist as words; the yield per language is not random but predictable from prosodic type, vowel-length contrast, and tone. This should be a documented catalog variable, not noise.

### 2. EXECUTION

**Databases to model on or reuse.** ASJP (Wichmann et al., *ASJP Database*; v21 2025 via lexibank/asjp) — 40-item basic-vocabulary lists in simplified ASJPcode, 11,540 wordlists linked to 6,126 Glottocodes; the canonical resource for automated lexical comparison, and it uses Levenshtein distance for similarity. Lexibank (above) is the richest reusable CLDF corpus. Others named in the spec and confirmed useful: NorthEuraLex (IPA, Northern Eurasia), IDS (Intercontinental Dictionary Series), World Loanword Database (WOLD), CELEX (rich phonological/frequency data for English/Dutch/German, used by Trott & Bergen), and WikiPron for mined pronunciations. CLICS³ (Rzymski et al. 2020, *Scientific Data*) is the model for colexification.

**Workflow/tooling.** The reproducible pipeline is: raw source → PyLexibank/CLDFBench (Forkel & List 2020, LREC) → CLDF dataset (linked to Glottolog/Concepticon/CLTS) → CL Toolkit feature extraction → analysis in LingPy/Python/R (rcldf). This stack directly delivers FAIR data. LingPy (List & Forkel) is the workhorse for sequence comparison, alignment, and cognate detection.

**Quality control.** Best practices from this literature: provenance tracking to the "doculect" (source document) level, as in PHOIBLE where multiple conflicting inventories per language are kept rather than merged; inter-annotator/inter-source agreement checks; explicit handling of dialectal variation (WikiPron's `--dialect` flag; PHOIBLE's multiple inventories). For language sampling, the field standard is genealogical + areal balancing: Easterday (2019) balances by family and macro-area; WALS/Grambank use structured sampling; Bickel's (2008) refined genealogical-control sampling is the methodological reference. The CLICS team also demonstrated crowd/student validation of data-creation workflows.

### 3. ANALYSIS

**Descriptive (fits original scope).** Phonotactic inventories and syllable-shape frequency distributions; onset-cluster typology (V/CV/CCV/CCCV counts); onset×nucleus co-occurrence grids and gap analysis (the Fudge 1969/1987 and Kessler & Treiman tradition for English, generalizable); functional load of open monosyllables; and the relationship between monosyllable inventory size, phoneme-inventory size, and tone. The tonal/monosyllabic connection is well-attested: Vietnamese and Sinitic languages are canonically monosyllabic-morpheme + tonal, with tightly constrained syllable templates (onset + optional glide + obligatory vowel + optional coda + tone). As Nguyen et al. (arXiv:1904.05569) report, "the total number of unique syllables in Vietnamese is 19000 but there are only 6500 syllables used in practice" — exactly the kind of phonotactic-gap statistic this catalog produces.

**Lexical statistics (mostly fits scope; some needs frequency data).** Zipf's law and word length; Piantadosi, Tily & Gibson (2011, PNAS 108:3526–3529) showing information content predicts word length better than frequency — directly relevant because open monosyllables are the shortest, highest-frequency, most information-dense forms. Homophony: Trott & Bergen (2020, *Cognition*) find real lexica have *fewer* homophones than phonotactically-matched baselines (i.e., possible evidence of homophony avoidance), with the debate continued by Pimentel et al. (2021, EMNLP, Rényi entropy) and the older Sampson counterexample. Reported homophony rates vary by source and measurement: on average "roughly 4% of the words in a language are estimated to be homophones" (Dautriche 2015); for English, Rodd et al. (2002) estimate 7.4%, while Trott & Bergen (2020) cite figures up to ~15% of English wordforms (as summarized in Pimentel et al. 2021, "On Homophony and Rényi Entropy," arXiv:2109.13766). Open monosyllabic lexicons are precisely where homophony concentrates, so this is a natural analysis — but requires frequency/meaning data beyond the base spec.

**Comparative/quantitative (requires relaxing the original non-goals).** Phonetic string similarity: normalized edit distance (NED), and sound-class methods — Dolgopolsky (1964) sound classes, List's SCA (Sound-Class-Based Alignment, 2012) and LexStat (2012), all in LingPy; ASJP uses Levenshtein. Automated cognate detection (LexStat-Infomap; List, Greenhill & Gray 2017 on its reliability) works but is calibrated on wordlists, not on isolated monosyllables. Colexification via CLICS (network approach; community detection). Sound symbolism/iconicity: Blasi, Wichmann, Hammarström, Stadler & Christiansen (2016, PNAS 113(39):10818–10823), "Sound–meaning association biases evidenced across thousands of languages," report: "By analyzing word lists covering nearly two-thirds of the world's languages, we demonstrate that a considerable proportion of 100 basic vocabulary items carry strong associations with specific kinds of human speech sounds... property words ('small' and i, 'full' and p or b) and body part terms ('tongue' and l, 'nose' and n)." Follow-ups (Pimentel et al.; Kawahara & Moore) refine the (small but real) effect size. The bouba/kiki literature (Köhler 1929; Ramachandran & Hubbard 2001) is the experimental complement.

**The chance-resemblance problem (critical caveat).** Ringe (1992), *On Calculating the Factor of Chance in Language Comparison* (Transactions of the American Philosophical Society 82:1), is the foundational treatment: short words drawn from small phoneme sets produce accidental look-alikes at high rates, which is why mass comparison (Greenberg) is statistically unreliable. Tresoldi's (2019) computational chance-resemblance work shows, e.g., that for CV shapes with small inventories and 100-item lists, one or more "perfect" matches by chance is highly probable. Because this catalog is *specifically* about the shortest possible words (open monosyllables, often CV), it sits at the worst point of the chance-resemblance curve. Any comparative similarity claim must be tested against an explicit null model (phonotactically-informed, à la Trott & Bergen / Ringe / Kessler's multilateral-comparison metrics).

**Psycholinguistic angles.** Phonotactic probability and neighborhood density (Vitevitch & Luce 1999, *Journal of Memory and Language* 40:374–408) — positively correlated: "the analysis of 1,041 CVC words showed that the sum of the frequency of the segments in each word was positively correlated with the neighborhood density of the word (r = .61; p < .0001)," with opposing processing effects (dense neighborhoods slow recognition but high phonotactic probability facilitates it); open monosyllables are typically in dense neighborhoods. CV as the universal/default/unmarked syllable in acquisition and typology (Jakobson; Blevins) grounds the theoretical significance of cataloging exactly the open-CV space.

### 4. VISUALIZATION

**Static (matches user preference).** Syllable-structure tree diagrams (onset + rime[nucleus + coda]) grounded in Clements & Keyser (1983) *CV Phonology* and Fudge (1969); IPA vowel/consonant charts per the IPA Handbook (1999); onset×nucleus phonotactic grids/heatmaps in the Fudge/Kessler & Treiman tradition (typically custom matplotlib/ggplot — no single canonical tool); frequency bar charts of syllable-shape distributions; and WALS-style typological feature maps. For maps specifically, the reusable tooling is CLDFViz (`cldfviz.map`; Forkel 2021, Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.5221554), which renders both interactive Leaflet maps and *static* PNG/JPG/PDF/SVG maps via cartopy with real projections (e.g., Mollweide) — ideal given the static preference. The R package lingtypology (Moroz) produces WALS/Glottolog-style maps and pulls WALS/AUTOTYP/PHOIBLE data directly.

**Computational/interactive (excluded by user preference, included for completeness).** CLICS colexification network graphs are the flagship: Mayer, List, Terhalle & Urban (2014, VisLR@LREC) built force-directed graphs in D3.js, with Infomap community detection (Rosvall & Bergstrom 2008, PNAS 105:1118–1123) partitioning the network into semantic-field clusters; CLICS³ (Rzymski et al. 2020) scaled this to 30 datasets / 3,156 varieties / 2,906 concepts. Online database interfaces (PHOIBLE, WALS, Glottolog, CLICS) all run on the clld framework with Leaflet maps. Best-practice how-to guidance exists: List & Forkel (2021, "How to visualize colexification networks with JavaScript and D3," *CALC in Practice*) and Tjuka (2024, "How to visualize colexification networks in Cytoscape," *CALC in Practice* 7(1):7–16). CLDFViz also has a `cldfviz.network` subcommand exporting to Graphviz DOT.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the descriptive catalog on standard rails (fits original scope, low risk).**
1. Adopt CLDF as the storage format and link every language to Glottolog, every gloss to Concepticon, every sound to CLTS. Use PyLexibank/CLDFBench so the output is FAIR and reusable.
2. Source IPA per language in priority order: existing Lexibank/NorthEuraLex CLDF data → WikiPron/Wiktextract mined pronunciations → Epitran G2P only where necessary, flagging G2P-derived forms as lower-confidence. Validate the resulting segment inventory against PHOIBLE for that language.
3. Tokenize with `segments`/orthography profiles; fix the transcription level at phonemic BroadIPA; and *write down* your diphthong-vs-VG, syllabic-consonant, tone, and nasality decisions as explicit, versioned rules. This is the single highest-leverage quality step.
4. Record word-minimality/prosodic type as a first-class variable per language (does it permit open light monosyllables? require bimoraicity?). This turns your uneven per-language yield from a bug into a finding.
5. Visualize statically: per-language onset×nucleus heatmaps, syllable-shape bar charts, and (if you want cross-language maps) CLDFViz static map output.

**Stage 2 — Add same-language quantitative analyses (small scope expansion, moderate effort).** Homophony rates, phonotactic probability/neighborhood density, and Zipf/Piantadosi word-length–information relationships. These need frequency and full-lexicon data (CELEX-style) but stay within single languages, avoiding the chance-resemblance trap.

**Stage 3 — Comparative/quantitative (relaxes original non-goals; proceed only with baselines).** If you want cross-linguistic similarity, colexification, sound-symbolism, or cognate signals: use LingPy SCA/LexStat (not raw orthographic edit distance), reuse ASJP/CLICS infrastructure, and — non-negotiably — pair every similarity claim with a chance-resemblance null model (Ringe 1992; Tresoldi 2019). Report effect sizes with the honest expectation that for open monosyllables they will be small and easily swamped by chance.

**Thresholds that change the plan.** If a target language's WALS syllable class is "complex" or it enforces bimoraic minimality, expect a thin open-monosyllable yield — deprioritize or treat as a controlled contrast case. If G2P word-error rate for a language is high (deep orthography), do not trust automatically-derived "open" classifications without manual spot-checks. If comparative similarity scores do not exceed a phonotactic null baseline, report them as null results, not discoveries.

## Caveats
- **Chance resemblance dominates short-word comparison.** This is the defining methodological risk; open CV monosyllables are the worst case. Do not publish cross-linguistic look-alike claims without an explicit statistical baseline.
- **"Open monosyllable" is partly theory-dependent.** Whether a form counts depends on unresolved analytic choices (diphthong/glide, syllabic consonants); different but defensible frameworks yield different catalogs. Document, don't assume.
- **Source data is uneven and noisy.** Wiktionary-derived IPA and G2P output contain systematic errors that concentrate in exactly the vowel/coda region that defines openness; PHOIBLE validation and manual spot-checks are essential.
- **Per-language yield is structurally biased**, not random, because of minimality constraints and syllable typology — so raw cross-language counts of open monosyllables are not directly comparable without controlling for prosodic type.
- **Some cited statistics are version- and source-dependent** (WikiPron 1.7M/165 vs 3M+ pairs; PanLex 2014 vs later promotional figures; homophony rates 4%–15% depending on source and method); cite the peer-reviewed figure and the version you actually use.
